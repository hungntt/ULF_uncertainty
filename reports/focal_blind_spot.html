The finding

The model you pick on SSIM is not the model that keeps lesions

Six enhancers trained on the same 40 paired subjects, scored on the same locked ten. Lesions of known size and contrast are inserted into the 3T, carried through the forward operator, and measured in the output: retention is the fraction of a lesion's core-minus-ring contrast that survives.

Rank correlation between SSIM and retention: +0.03. The two rankings invert at both ends. The pipeline that is second-best on SSIM is the worst of six at keeping lesions; the one that keeps the most is fifth on SSIM.

Read the size of the effect carefully. These are one trained model per architecture. Three seeds each of U-Net L1 and pix2pix put the pix2pix figure above at the top of its range: the seed means are 0.372 ± 0.048 against 0.345 ± 0.009, so the margin falls from +0.073 to +0.027 and the gap is smaller than the spread within one architecture. The direction holds at every seed — SSIM prefers U-Net, retention prefers pix2pix — but the headline is the correlation, not the margin.
The obvious reply, tested

“Then evaluate on a downstream task.” It is worse.

The standard answer to a metric that fails is to replace it with a task. So: fit a three-component intensity mixture to the within-brain voxels of the real 3T, apply that same fitted model to the synthesised image, and measure tissue agreement. Fitting on the target makes this the easy version — the classifier is tuned to the truth, and only the synthesised intensities have to land in the right places.

The extremes state it without the statistics. The best pipeline on the downstream task is the worst of six on lesion retention — 45% of lesions erased. The best on retention is fifth of six on the task.

Same caveat as above: six pipelines, one run each, so a −0.43 coefficient at n = 6 is not significant and the weight sits on the extremes. The proxy is intensity-only and fitted on the real image, which makes it a floor-level task rather than a clinical one. Every pipeline beats the raw 64 mT input, so this is a finding about ranking, not about whether synthesis is worth doing.

Why

Focal and global accuracy are in tension

Both measures are averages over brain tissue. Measured directly over sampled training batches, an inserted lesion of radius 2–6 px covers 0.009–0.043% of the image — roughly one five-thousandth of an L1 gradient.

Smoothing harder improves bulk tissue agreement and erases more focal structure. That is a real trade, and a model sits somewhere on it. No global measure — hand-made or learned, image-level or task-level — reports which side.

So the argument is not that image metrics are crude. It is that a focal question needs a focal measure. Reporting a downstream task alongside SSIM adds a second view of the same 99.98% of the image, and in this cohort it is the more misleading of the two.
What to report instead

Predict erasure, not error

A U-Net trained on sparse supervision at inserted lesion sites, answering one counterfactual about the pipeline it audits: if a lesion of this size and contrast were here, would this synthesis erase it? Supervision is possible because the cohort is paired — 50 subjects with both a 64 mT and a 3T acquisition — so the error can be learned rather than guessed from model-internal signals.

The two uncertainty baselines are below chance — not uninformative but anti-correlated. Where the enhancer's intensity error is largest is where lesions are most likely to survive, because both track edges and texture. Predicting intensity error is the wrong question.

It is not a dressed-up edge detector

Radius is an input to the map, so the test that matters is within one size band. Held-out R² for seven hand-crafted local descriptors — gradient, variance, depth from the brain edge, intensity, a noise proxy, model change, contrast — against the map alone:

Does it survive contact with reality

Three ways it could have been an artefact of its own setup

1 · Lesion families it never saw

Trained on isotropic hyperintense Gaussians; tested on five families it was never shown. AUROC falls by 0.04–0.14 and stays well above chance throughout.

The hypointense row carries a clinical finding of its own: dark lesions are erased at twice the rate of bright ones. Retrained on both polarities the map reaches 0.914, so removing that restriction costs about 0.01 AUROC.

2 · Enhancers it never saw

One fixed map, scored against all six pipelines. AUROC 0.857–0.923, with the within-size figure almost flat at 0.78–0.80 — a spread no wider than its own seed noise. A map trained for one pipeline ranks sites correctly under five others.

3 · Real focal structure, not inserted blobs

Screening all 50 paired subjects for focal white-matter hyperintensities — excluding CSF, requiring a white-matter background and persistence across slices — leaves 27 distinct features in 9 of 50 subjects, about 5 mm, all periventricular. They are not confirmed pathology; they are real structure with real texture and edges, which is what the phantom stands in for.

Inpainting each feature out gives a lesion-free baseline. One arm adds the real feature back; the other adds a Gaussian matched on the feature's own fitted extent. Each subject is scored by the cross-fit enhancer from the fold that excluded it.

A Gaussian phantom predicts real-structure behaviour, provided it is matched on effective extent rather than on a thresholded core. Real focal features have flat cores and soft shoulders: their effective radius is about 1.5× the radius a threshold measures. Match on the core instead and the apparent gap inflates to +0.17–0.26 — which is where this result started, and what the profile fit explains away.

The map, trained entirely on phantoms, predicts erasure of these real features at 0.705 — 0.784 when asked about the feature's effective extent rather than its thresholded core. Both sit inside the within-size band it reaches on phantoms.

And the closed loop does not bite

Every number above feeds the enhancer a 64 mT image produced by a learned forward operator, which is the usual reason to distrust a study like this. The cohort has real 64 mT acquisitions, and the two label sets share 2934 identical sites — same slice, same centre, same radius, same contrast — differing only in which image the enhancer was given.

The deliverable

A size-stratified statement of what the synthesis can be trusted for

The map is conditional on the lesion it is asked about, so a single flagged fraction would be misleading. Conformal risk control turns it into a distribution-free guarantee: at a certified 15% missed-finding rate, the fraction of brain where a lesion of that size cannot be trusted.

lesion sizefaint (c = 0.12)clear (c = 0.25)
4 mm100.0%100.0%
6 mm80.6%63.2%
8 mm30.6%24.5%
12 mm3.6%3.9%

Read as a clinical statement: at a 15% guaranteed miss rate, a 4 mm lesion cannot be trusted anywhere in the image; a 12 mm lesion can be trusted in 96% of it. The 100% row is not the map failing — it is the map refusing to certify something the pipeline genuinely cannot do, since a 4 mm lesion retains about 17% of its contrast.

The size column is a Gaussian radius, not a calliper. Real focal structure behaves like an object about 1.5× wider than its suprathreshold diameter, so a finding a reader would measure at 4 mm sits nearer the 6 mm row. A specification handed to a radiologist needs a stated convention for that conversion, and the conversion is not the identity.
What it is not

It cannot compare models, and that is a property

The map's input includes the prediction of the pipeline it audits. Across six enhancers whose true retention spans 0.314–0.427, its mean prediction spans 0.317–0.326.

It generalises to unseen enhancers because it never depended on the enhancer. The conditioning was redundant by construction: with one enhancer in training, the prediction is a deterministic function of the input and carries nothing new, so nothing in the objective could have taught the map to read it.

Training a map across three enhancers, so that pipeline identity does carry signal, does not fix it — predicted spread 0.008 against an actual 0.113, the same ratio — and within-size accuracy falls. Two attempts have failed, so this is a characterised property, not a gap awaiting a later version.

So describe it accurately. Not “a QA map for this pipeline”, but a map of where this class of synthesis loses lesions — a property of the site and the lesion. It must not be used to choose between models, which is the first thing a reader would try.
A third instance of the same blindness

The T2-driven route fabricates lesion polarity

Direction-2 found that 64 mT T2 predicts 3T T1 better than 64 mT T1 does, and on every image metric the T2-driven route wins. It is not transferring the lesion. It is applying the population-average T2→T1 coupling.

T1/T2 polarityretention% inverted
T1-dark, canonical0.6714%
T1-bright — haemorrhage, fat, contrast enhancement−0.38695%

For a lesion that violates the usual coupling, the reader is shown a dark lesion where the truth is bright, in a clean and plausible image, 95% of the time. That is worse than an erasure, and no population-level metric can see a per-lesion sign flip.

It survives both obvious repairs. Training with polarity-violating lesions changes nothing (88% → 87%); adding a lesion-weighted loss worth as much as the whole-brain term changes nothing either (86–91%), though it does cut spurious inversion on canonical lesions from 10% to 2%. The evidence is present — polarity is recoverable from the 64 mT T1 input at AUROC 0.972 — and the architecture hands it to the model through a skip connection, which the residual branch then cancels. The inversion is a consequence of a trade the model makes everywhere else, which is why flagging it is the near-term answer rather than training it away.

Limits

What would have to be true before any of this is clinical

  • No clinician has read anything here. The 27 real features are unconfirmed, all periventricular, all about 5 mm. Intensity error has already been shown to be a poor proxy for clinical salience, and nothing replaces a radiologist.
  • One site, ten locked test subjects, one field-strength pair. Every number is from a single cohort of 50 paired scans.
  • The headline correlations rest on six pipelines, one trained model each. A seed study on two of them cut the architecture margin by more than half; the same exercise for the downstream-task result is running, and until it lands the weight sits on the extremes rather than on the coefficients.
  • Lesions are simulated, and the validation of that is narrow. The phantom reproduces real-structure retention to within 0.023, but across 27 features in one size band and one location.
  • The guarantee is calibrated per pipeline on phantom lesions. It should not be quoted for real structure without its own calibration set.
What this is. A methods and evaluation study: the claim is about how ULF enhancement is measured, backed by a controlled comparison and a measure that is validated against real structure. It is not a clinical study and does not read as one.
